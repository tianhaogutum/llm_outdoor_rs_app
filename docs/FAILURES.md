# Failure analysis: the ten worst queries of the served default

The ten test personas on which the served default `recsys_serve` (DECISIONS.md D25) has the lowest NDCG@10,
what it returned, where it lost the routes the judge liked best, and why. The data blocks are generated by
`make failures` (which replays each request through the pipeline from the frozen cache and checks that the
replay returns exactly the stored ranking; no credentials) and rendered by `make report` from
`eval/results/_failures.json`. The diagnoses are written by hand after reading the stage traces and the judge's
reasons. `make replay-check` recomputes the analysis and fails if it no longer matches the committed file.

Read every grade below with the usual caveat: the users are simulated, the grades come from an LLM judge that
has not been checked against the owner's labels, and the judge reads a latent profile that the system never
sees (EVAL.md sections 2-5). "Lost at" names the stage after which a route was no longer in the top 10:
`retrieval` (eligible but not in the fused top 50), `ranker` (a candidate the learned ranker put below 10),
`mmr`, `exploration`, or `filter` (never happens for a relevant route here: violating pairs are graded 0).

**One bias to keep in mind when reading the "other systems" columns.** These ten are selected for being the
served system's worst. Any other system whose errors are not identical will look better on them by regression
to the mean, so "`v4_rrf` beats the served system here" is weak evidence on its own. The stage traces are the
evidence; the other columns only point at where to look.

- **A. The learned ranker overrides retrieval for a stated minority need.** Retrieval found the right routes;
  the ranker applied a prior learned from the majority of training personas (higher technical grade is bad, short
  is bad, every requested concept counts the same) to a user for whom it is wrong, because no feature lets the
  stated need win (stroller suitability, technical skill, train access, an upper bound on length) and the
  training split has few such personas. This is the largest class and the one the system owns.
- **B. Length the served pipeline cannot read.** A vague intent with no distance, or a duration in days. The
  served ranker gave up `length_cap_ratio` (D19) and has nothing else; the ranker that kept it does better on both
  queries, which is the measured cost of the D19 choice on the queries where it matters. The fix is a real
  comfortable-distance slot and day parsing, not putting the simulator-shaped feature back.
- **C. The evaluation's fault: the judge grades wishes the user never stated.** Train travel, a one-way shape,
  a lake: the latent profile has them, the intent writer left them out, the judge penalises their absence. No
  query-only system can satisfy these; they lower every system's NDCG for these personas.
- **D. Not a ranking failure.** A crowded field of good routes where the 2-versus-3 line depends on unstated
  preferences.

So about a third of the ten (C and D, and part of P110) are failures of the evaluation rather than the system.
That is consistent with EVAL.md section 5: intents are written from the latent profile but do not contain all of
it, and the judge reads all of it.

**What data or change would fix most of them, in order of expected effect:**

1. **Stated needs as constraints, not equal-weight concepts** (A, part of C): slots for stroller, arriving by
   train, technical skill and shape, each mapped to a catalogue field that exists (steps and stroller share,
   `Train access`, technical grade, `is_loop`), filtered like the radius when the user states them; upper bounds
   in the intent routed to the cap slot.
2. **Ranker features that can depend on the user** (A): grade minus stated skill, length relative to the stated
   or party-typical comfortable distance, concept weights by salience in the intent; more minority personas
   (MTB, stroller, train) in the training split, or real feedback from such users.
3. **A comfortable-distance slot and day parsing** (B), asked for when the intent is vague.
4. **Evaluation fixes** (C, D): intents that state every hard requirement of the latent profile, or a judge that
   grades only against what the request expresses plus the hard constraints; the owner's labels to check the
   judge's 2-versus-3 boundary.

**New failures.** Look at the queries that got worse, not at the mean. Three patterns, each visible in the traces:

- *The catalogue's check is stricter than the judge's reading.* "Reachable by train" is verified as a station
  within 1 km of the start; the judge also accepts a station at the end of a one-way route or a bus at the start
  (P019, P280: grade-2 and grade-3 routes with only a bus stop are filtered out). "Stroller" requires no steps at
  all; the judge forgives a few metres (P260).
- *Promotion trusts a need the relaxation had already given up.* For P157 ("a loop of around 30 km") too few loops
  of 15-45 km were in reach, so both needs were dropped, and promotion then put the five that meet them on top,

- *A length window reshuffles retrieval.* For P033, P117 and P271 the stated length was read correctly and most
  relevant routes meet it, but filtering changes the candidate set the fusion and the ranker see, and the order
  of the top 10 moves by a few judged routes. These losses are of the size of the ranker's ordinary noise.

And the MTB need is weaker evidence than it looks: for MTB personas the judge often grades paved cycling routes 2
(P108), so promoting mapped MTB routes can cost as much as it gains. The analysed variant's own ten worst queries
lose two dozen relevant routes at the needs stage, which is the cost side of the same coin.
